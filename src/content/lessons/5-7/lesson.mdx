## Where did that number come from?

A research system's report says "the market grew 18% last year." Which source says that? Is it the same source that reported 12% elsewhere in the report? Was the 18% measured last year or three years ago? If the report can't answer these questions, readers can't trust it, however polished it is.

The last task statement in Domain 5, 5.6, is about **provenance** (keeping every claim tied to its source through the whole pipeline) and **uncertainty** (reporting conflicts and gaps honestly instead of smoothing them over).

## How attribution gets lost

In a multi-agent research pipeline, findings pass through several hands: search subagents, the coordinator, a synthesis agent, sometimes a summarizer. Each step compresses. The guide names the failure: **source attribution is lost during summarization when findings are compressed without preserving claim-source mappings.**

```text
Subagent:   "Heat-pump sales rose 31% in 2025 (Northwind
            Energy Review 2026, p.14)."
Summary 1:  "Heat-pump sales rose about 30% last year."
Summary 2:  "Heat-pump adoption is growing rapidly."
```

Each step is individually reasonable, and by the end the claim is unattributable and vague. Lesson 1.5 introduced the fix for handoffs; this lesson extends it through synthesis.

## Structured claim-source mappings

Require subagents to output **structured claim-source mappings**, including **source URLs, document names and relevant excerpts**, and require downstream agents to **preserve and merge them** through synthesis:

```json
{
  "claim": "Heat-pump sales rose 31% in 2025",
  "source": "Northwind Energy Review 2026",
  "url": "https://example.org/northwind-review-2026",
  "excerpt": "sales grew 31% year on year in 2025",
  "date": "2026-04",
  "method": "national registration data, 40 markets"
}
```

The synthesis agent's instructions then say: **every claim in the report keeps its mapping**; when claims are merged, their sources are merged too, never dropped. The report can show citations inline, and anyone can check a claim against its excerpt.

## Conflicting numbers: annotate, don't pick

Credible sources disagree all the time. One analyst reports 18% growth and another reports 12%. The tempting move is to pick one: the bigger publisher, the more recent one, or the round-number average. The guide says: **annotate conflicts with source attribution rather than arbitrarily selecting one value.**

```text
Market growth, 2025:
- 18% (Analyst A, 2026-02; revenue-based, incl. services)
- 12% (Analyst B, 2026-01; unit shipments only)
The difference likely reflects the metric: revenue vs units.
```

Now the reader sees both values, who reported them, and the plausible reason they differ. Often the "conflict" is two different measurements.

The same applies earlier in the pipeline: a document-analysis step should **complete its analysis with the conflicting values included and explicitly annotated**, and **let the coordinator decide how to reconcile them** before passing anything to synthesis. A subagent that silently picks one value has made an editorial decision it wasn't asked to make, and the evidence for the other value is gone.

## Dates prevent false contradictions

Some "conflicts" aren't conflicts at all. A 2021 source says unemployment was 6.1%; a 2025 source says 4.2%. Without dates, that looks like two sources disagreeing. With dates, it's a trend. The guide: **require publication or data collection dates in structured outputs**, so **temporal differences aren't misread as contradictions.** Note both dates when they differ: a 2025 report can describe 2023 data.

## Structure the report around certainty

A good synthesis report makes its certainty visible. The guide's practice: **explicit sections distinguishing well-established findings from contested ones**, **preserving the original sources' characterizations and methodological context**:

```text
## Well-established
- Heat-pump sales grew roughly 30% in 2025 (Northwind;
  Halden Institute; national registries). Three independent
  sources, consistent methods.

## Contested
- 2026 growth forecasts range from 15% to 28%. Sources
  differ in subsidy assumptions (see table).

## Gaps
- No recent data for Africa or South America (sources
  unavailable; see coverage notes).
```

"Preserving characterizations" matters too. If a study says its result is "preliminary" or "based on a small sample," the report keeps that qualifier instead of promoting the finding to established fact.

## Render each kind of content in its natural form

Synthesis often flattens everything into uniform paragraphs. The guide recommends **rendering different content types appropriately**:

- **Financial data as tables**: numbers side by side, with units and periods.
- **News and narrative as prose**: sequence and context matter.
- **Technical findings as structured lists**: discrete, checkable items.

A table of five companies' quarterly revenue is readable at a glance; the same data as prose is a paragraph of numbers nobody can compare.

## How this shows up on the exam

- **"The final report's claims can't be traced to sources"** → subagents output **claim-source mappings** (URL, document name, excerpt) that synthesis **preserves and merges**.
- **"Two credible sources give different statistics"** → include **both, annotated with their sources**; don't pick one arbitrarily.
- **"A subagent resolved a conflict on its own"** → include conflicting values with annotations and **let the coordinator reconcile**.
- **"Old and new figures look contradictory"** → require **publication or collection dates**.
- **"Readers can't tell solid findings from disputed ones"** → **established vs contested sections**, keeping the sources' own qualifiers.
- **"Everything is prose, including financial data"** → render **tables for numbers, prose for news, lists for technical findings**.
